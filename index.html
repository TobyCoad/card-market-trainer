The game

Rules

Cards are turned one at a time. Before each new card you bet a fraction of your bankroll on whether it will be higher or lower than the card showing. You start with €1000 and play until the deck runs out — 12 bets with a 13-card deck. Every card already turned stays visible, so nothing is hidden from you.

The one rule you need

Count what is left. If the card showing is c, and of the n unseen cards h are higher and l are lower, then bet

|h − l| / n of your bankroll on the majority side.

That is just Kelly: for an even-money bet the growth-optimal stake is f = p − q = 2p − 1, and here p = max(h,l)/n, so 2p − 1 = |h − l|/n. You never have to remember a formula — you read it straight off the count.

Why full Kelly is safe here

Normally you bet a fraction of Kelly because your probabilities are estimates. Here they are counted facts, not a model. And the stake reaches 100% only when h or l is zero — that is, only when the win is certain. So full Kelly in this game can never bust you. Say that out loud; it is the sharpest observation available about the sizing.

First bet, by the card showing

With 12 unseen the stake is |14 − 2c| / 12: A or K 100% · 2 or Q 83% · 3 or J 67% · 4 or 10 50% · 5 or 9 33% · 6 or 8 17% · 7 no edge, pass.

What to expect

Over 200,000 simulated games, full Kelly ends with a median around €55,000 and a mean near €100,000, and finishes below the starting €1000 only about 0.2% of the time. You get on average 2(H13 − 1) ≈ 4.4 certain-win rounds per game — free doublings, because as the deck empties the card showing is often the highest or lowest left. Typical growth is about 5.7 doublings.

The trap worth naming

Betting everything whenever you have any edge has a higher expected value (about €213,000) but a median of zero — it busts 89% of the time. Maximising E[wealth] and E[log wealth] give completely different answers, and Kelly targets the growth rate that survives repetition. Flat-betting 25% every round is far worse than Kelly too, at a median around €5,200.

Doing it on a clock

Interview mode hides your bankroll, turns the hints off and puts three clocks on you: 10 seconds a decision, 5 minutes for the hand, and 15 seconds to state your final P&L. Run out of hand clock and you are stopped mid-deck and asked for your number anyway.

The stake takes almost no thought once you trust |h − l| / n — the time goes on second-guessing a count you already have. Keep the running bankroll updated as each card turns, not at the end: the P&L question should be you reading out a number you are already holding, inside about 8 seconds. Reconstructing twelve rounds from memory under a clock is where candidates come apart.

Ask before you start

Are ties possible (a 52-card deck) and is a tie a push or a loss? Is it even money? Must you bet every round or can you pass at zero edge? Is the comparison always against the most recent card? Fifteen seconds of clarifying questions shows exactly the instinct being tested.
