The game

Rules, as played in interviews

Three cards are dealt face down from one 52-card deck and you're quoted a market on their sum, e.g. 24 at 28. You work out what the sum is worth, compare it to the quote and hit the side that's off: buy if fair is above the offer, sell if it's below the bid, pass if fair sits inside the spread. Then you pick a size. The cards flash face up for a couple of seconds and flip back, and you have to state your own P&L from memory. You start with €1000. Cards do not go back in the deck.

Baseline fair value

With ace = 1 each card averages 7, so three cards average 21. With ace = 14 each averages 8, so 24. The sum's standard deviation is about 6.5 (one card ~3.7, times √3) — landing a few points off fair is normal, so a single loss tells you nothing about whether the trade was right.

Full-deck sums: 4 × (1+2+…+13) = 364 with ace low; 416 with ace high.

Why you can't just say 21 forever

Fair value is always cards per round × (sum of cards still in the deck) ÷ (cards still in the deck). Seen cards are gone. Example: you've seen 15 cards averaging 9. Remaining sum = 364 − 135 = 229 over 37 cards = 6.19 per card, so fair = 18.6, not 21 — a bigger move than most of the mispricing in the quotes. Keep a running count and sum; every card above the current mean drags fair down, every card below pulls it up.

Edge and sizing

Edge = fair − offer when buying, bid − fair when selling. Size should scale with edge: a one-point edge gets a small clip, a five-point edge gets near your max. This app's "suggested size" is max size × edge ÷ (one standard deviation of the sum), capped — a rule of thumb, not gospel; what's graded is that bigger edges get bigger sizes.

P&L from memory

Bought at the offer A with size s: P&L = (sum − A) × s. Sold at the bid B: P&L = (B − sum) × s. Sign discipline matters more than speed — a losing trade reported as a win is the classic miss. Remember the cards as a sum, not as three faces.

"How would the game change if ace is high?"

A reported follow-up. Fair rises from 21 to 24 (deck sum 416); per-card spread widens slightly (values 2–14 instead of 1–13, but the same 13 equally likely values so the standard deviation is identical); your running count must use the new values for every ace seen and for the aces still in the deck. If the rule switches mid-game, recompute the remaining-deck sum immediately — each unseen ace is now worth 13 more.
